## Section 1

### Transmission-linked parasite pairs retain only partial genealogical separation, establishing a biological ceiling on detectable signal

Before any inference layer is applied, the most fundamental question for transmission detectability is whether the biological system produces meaningfully distinct genealogical signals between directly linked and unrelated parasite pairs. We addressed this by examining the distribution of true coalescence times (TMRCA) between transmission-linked pairs (direct descendants within a defined generational window, G ≤ 25) and epidemiologically unrelated pairs (non-direct) under baseline simulation parameters.

Directly transmitted parasite pairs were strongly concentrated at shallow genealogical depths, with the majority of direct pairs coalescing within one to three generations of the sampled timepoint (Figure 1, left panel). Non-direct pairs were broadly dispersed across the 0–50 generation window, reflecting the heterogeneous mixture of recently diverged lineages and more distantly related background haplotypes that characterises an endemic parasite population. Critically, the two distributions overlap substantially within the epidemiologically relevant transmission window, particularly between G = 3 and G = 15, where non-direct pairs contribute a non-trivial density that cannot be excluded from any genomic inference applied at this generational resolution. This overlap defines the absolute upper bound on any measure of transmission detectability: even with perfect genealogical knowledge, same-generation pairs cannot be completely separated from background relatedness by time to common ancestry alone.

Under the recombination sweep condition — in which recombination rate was elevated across the parameter range tested — the distribution of non-direct TMRCA values was modestly widened relative to baseline, with more mass at intermediate depths (Figure 1, right panel). The distribution of direct pairs was not substantially altered by the sweep, as direct transmission relationships are determined by the pedigree structure of the simulation and are independent of recombination at the genealogical level. However, the consequence of the widened non-direct distribution was a slight compression of the distributional gap between classes, reducing the biological separation available to any downstream inference approach. Together, these results establish that the detectable genealogical signal between transmission-linked and unrelated pairs is an incomplete, biologically constrained quantity — not a cleanly separable property — and that the ceiling on inference performance is set before any analytical choice is made.

> **Figure 1.** True coalescence time (TMRCA) distributions for directly and non-directly transmitted parasite pair classes. Left: baseline simulation parameters. Right: recombination sweep condition. In both panels, direct pairs (red) are concentrated at shallow genealogical depths (G \< 5), while non-direct pairs (grey) are broadly dispersed across the full window. Overlap in the G = 3–15 range is present under both conditions and is not eliminated by any parameter setting examined. The overlap region represents the irreducible biological floor on transmission detectability — the fraction of genealogical signal that is inherently ambiguous regardless of inference approach.

*Supplementary Figure S1* provides the full distribution of pairwise IBD fractions at baseline, showing both true and hmmIBD-inferred values across the complete set of transmission-linked and unlinked pairs. The bimodal structure visible in the true IBD distribution — a peak at high IBD values for direct pairs and a broad background peak for non-direct pairs — confirms that the genealogical signal characterised here is partially recoverable in sequence data, but is compressed by the overlapping tails of each distribution.

## Section 2

### Recombination progressively erodes the genomic signal of shared ancestry, widening the gap between available and recoverable transmission information

Given that direct and non-direct pairs are partially separable at the genealogical level, we next characterised how recombination transforms this biological signal into observable genomic patterns. Two gaps are relevant here: the decay of the true genealogical signal itself as recombination fragments shared haplotype blocks, and the growing divergence between what the genome contains and what can be recovered from sequence data by an inference method.

As recombination rate increased across the five levels examined (r = $10^{-9}$ to r = $10^{-6}$ per base pair per generation), mean true IBD proportions declined monotonically for both direct and non-direct pairs (Figure 2A). At the lowest recombination rate (r = $10^{-9}$), direct pairs retained high mean IBD proportions, reflecting long unbroken haplotype blocks inherited across the transmission chain. At the highest rate (r = $10^{-6}$), even directly transmitted pairs showed markedly reduced mean IBD, as recombination during meiosis in the mosquito midgut fragmented shared ancestry into segments too short to be unambiguously distinguished from the background distribution of non-direct pairs. This decay of the available signal constitutes a primary biological constraint: regardless of how the data are analysed, less information is available at higher recombination rates.

Inferred IBD fractions, estimated by hmmIBD from sequence data, tracked the true signal closely at intermediate recombination rates but showed a growing divergence from ground truth at both extremes of the tested range (Figure 2B, 2C). At r = $10^{-9}$, inferred IBD was elevated relative to true IBD for non-direct pairs — a consequence of the long-range linkage disequilibrium that accumulates at very low recombination rates, which causes hmmIBD to classify background identity-by-state as identity by descent. At r = $10^{-6}$, inferred IBD declined faster than true IBD for direct pairs, as segment fragmentation pushed shared blocks below the minimum length threshold reliably detectable by the hidden Markov model. The divergence between the two curves at each end of the recombination spectrum defines a recovery gap: the fraction of available transmission signal that cannot be recovered from sequence data under realistic inference conditions.

The relationship between true and inferred IBD was non-monotonic across the recombination rate range (Figure 2D). The correlation between true and inferred IBD was lowest at r = $10^{-9}$ (Pearson r ≈ 0.53, 95% CI 0.49–0.58), peaked at r = $10^{-7}$ (r ≈ 0.95), and declined again at r = $10^{-6}$ (r ≈ 0.65). This non-monotonic profile reflects two distinct mechanisms of signal degradation operating at opposite ends of the recombination range: at low recombination, long shared segments cause inference over-sensitivity that conflates background relatedness with true IBD; at high recombination, segment fragmentation causes inference under-sensitivity that misses genuine but short shared ancestry. The intermediate recombination regime (r ≈ $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-6}$) — which encompasses the empirical P. falciparum recombination rate of approximately 7.4 × $10^{-7}$ bp\^-1 per meiosis — represents the region in which the inference layer most faithfully tracks available biological signal.

> **Figure 2.** Effect of recombination rate on the availability and recoverability of transmission signal. **(A)** Distribution of true IBD proportions for direct (red) and non-direct (grey) pairs across five recombination rates — the available signal ceiling at each rate. **(B)** Inferred IBD proportions (hmmIBD) at the same rates — the recoverable signal floor. **(C)** Divergence between mean true and inferred IBD as a function of recombination rate, illustrating the recovery gap. **(D)** Pearson correlation between true and inferred IBD, showing the non-monotonic recovery efficiency curve that peaks at r ≈ $10^{-7}$ and declines at both extremes. Note that panels A and B represent two layers of the same biological system — what genome biology provides and what sequence-based inference can recover from it — not a comparison between methods.

## Section 3 ★

### Transmission detection undergoes a threshold transition governed by recombination rate, independent of inference approach

The decay of signal characterised in Section 2 suggests that transmission detectability might collapse sharply at some recombination threshold rather than declining gradually across the full range. We tested this directly by computing balanced detection performance (F1 score) as a function of recombination rate for all three inference approaches — IBD (hmmIBD), IBS, and phylogenetic cophenetic distance — across the full parameter space.

Transmission detection exhibited a pronounced threshold-like transition rather than a gradual gradient (Figure 3A). Below r = $10^{-8}$, F1 scores for all three approaches were near zero (0.02–0.07 across methods and parameter combinations), reflecting an inability to simultaneously achieve meaningful precision and recall regardless of the classification threshold applied. No method, at any sample size, migration rate, or generational depth tested, produced F1 \> 0.10 under these conditions. Above r = 6.67 × $10^{-7}$, performance rose sharply: IBD reached F1 ≈ 0.54 and IBS reached F1 ≈ 0.48 at baseline parameters, while phylogenetic approaches showed partial improvement but did not reach a clearly identifiable regime across the tested conditions. The transition between these two states occurred within approximately one order of magnitude of recombination rate (between $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-7}$), and its location was invariant to migration rate and sample size — confirming that it reflects a biological threshold in signal availability rather than a statistical threshold in inference sensitivity.

The universality of the detection collapse below r = $10^{-8}$ is the defining feature of this result. A method-specific failure at low recombination would suggest that a different inference approach could overcome the barrier. Instead, all three approaches — which represent fundamentally different statistical paradigms, from segment-based IBD estimation to allele-sharing summary statistics to maximum-likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction — failed synchronously and to a similar degree below the threshold. This synchrony is consistent with the genealogical analysis in Section 1: when TMRCA distributions of direct and non-direct pairs overlap substantially, no representation of that genealogy in sequence data — IBD fractions, allele-sharing proportions, or tree topology — can recover sufficient discriminative signal to exceed baseline performance. The ceiling is set by the biology, not by the analysis.

In terms of precision-recall performance, the achievable envelope — the outer boundary of the precision-recall space accessible under the best conditions tested — contracted markedly from the baseline to the recombination sweep scenario (Figure 3B). Under baseline parameters, IBD approached moderate precision across the low-recall range, consistent with its ability to identify the closest relatives among transmission-linked pairs with reasonable specificity. Under the sweep scenario, even the upper-left corner of the precision-recall space contracted, indicating that high-precision identification of any transmission-linked pair became substantially more difficult. IBS showed intermediate contraction and phylogenetic approaches showed the steepest contraction, suggesting differential sensitivity to the recombination regime across signal types. Importantly, the envelope rather than the individual method curves is the relevant quantity at this juncture: it characterises how much of the precision-recall space is accessible to any approach under the given biological conditions, independent of methodological choice.

> **Figure 3.** Recombination-governed phase transition in transmission detection. **(A)** F1 score as a function of recombination rate for all three inference approaches (IBD: red, IBS: blue, Phylo: green) under baseline parameters. The near-zero plateau at r ≤ $10^{-8}$ and sharp rise above r = $10^{-7}$ define the biological detection threshold. The synchronous collapse of all approaches below this threshold indicates a biological rather than methodological limit. **(B)** Precision–recall curves for all three approaches under baseline, recombination sweep, and full factorial conditions, presented as the outer achievable precision–recall envelope rather than a method comparison. The progressive contraction of this envelope with increasing recombination characterises the shrinking of accessible precision-recall space as the biological signal degrades.

Expressed in terms of AUPRC — the primary summary metric, which accounts for class imbalance — the detection floor at the lowest recombination rate tested was absolute: no method exceeded AUPRC = 0.62 under any demographic or surveillance configuration at r = $10^{-9}$ (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S2). At the highest recombination rate (r = $10^{-6}$) and smallest sample size (n = 100), best AUPRC reached 0.79–0.82. However, this upper range contracted substantially with increasing sample size, with no condition exceeding AUPRC = 0.80 at n ≥ 200, a finding we address in detail in Section 6. *Supplementary Figure S4* shows the fraction of simulation replicates achieving identifiable status (AUPRC \> 0.70) across the parameter space, confirming that identifiable performance is concentrated at the intersection of high recombination rate and small sample sizes. *Supplementary Table S2* provides complete AUPRC values with 95% confidence intervals for all method-scenario combinations across the full factorial design.

## Section 4

### The detection threshold reflects coalescent geometry: genealogical overlap between transmission classes propagates into and is amplified by sequence-based representations

The preceding sections established that a threshold exists in transmission detectability governed by recombination rate, and that it manifests simultaneously across all inference approaches. We next sought to explain mechanistically why this threshold is located where it is and why phylogenetic approaches fail earlier and more completely than IBD-based inference. The explanation lies in how different sequence representations transform the genealogical overlap characterised in Section 1.

Pairwise cophenetic distances derived from the maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree showed distributions for direct and non-direct pairs that largely mirrored the TMRCA distributions in shape but with consistently greater overlap (Figure 4, left panel). Where TMRCA distributions showed partial separation between transmission classes, cophenetic distances showed near-complete overlap under baseline conditions. This amplification of overlap is a direct consequence of how phylogenetic reconstruction integrates genealogical signal across the recombining chromosome: because IQ-TREE reconstructs a single bifurcating tree from a genome that contains a mosaic of distinct genealogical histories, the resulting cophenetic distances reflect a recombination-averaged signal in which transmission-relevant ancestry is diluted by the background of unrelated loci occupying the same chromosome. The degree of dilution increases with recombination rate, as the proportion of the chromosome sharing the same genealogical history as any given locus decreases.

Under the recombination sweep condition, the cophenetic distance distributions for direct and non-direct pairs converged further, producing near-indistinguishable overlapping distributions (Figure 4, right panel). This convergence was markedly more pronounced than the corresponding convergence in TMRCA distributions (Figure 1, right panel), confirming that the phylogenetic representation amplifies rather than preserves the genealogical overlap present at ground truth. At the biological level, the sweep creates longer-range linkage disequilibrium across the chromosome, which causes the phylogenetic tree to be dominated by the most recent common ancestral signal at selected loci rather than the transmission-relevant genealogy at causal loci. The net result is that the cophenetic distance distribution of non-direct pairs is shifted toward smaller values — toward the direct pair distribution — making class separation more difficult precisely where it is already most challenging.

This mechanistic analysis explains the observation in Section 3 that phylogenetic approaches fail to achieve identifiable performance across all parameter combinations tested. It is not that the IQ-TREE implementation is suboptimal for this application. Rather, the cophenetic distance representation systematically loses genealogical resolution relative to ground truth as recombination increases, because it averages heterogeneous genealogical signals across the chromosome in a way that IBD segment detection does not. IBD segment-based inference retains local genealogical resolution by identifying blocks of shared ancestry at specific genomic positions, and is therefore less sensitive to the averaging effect that degrades phylogenetic signal. The differential robustness of IBD relative to phylogenetic approaches is thus a structural consequence of the recombining nature of the P. falciparum genome, not a property specific to any particular implementation.

> **Figure 4.** Genealogical basis of the detection threshold: cophenetic distance distributions mirror and amplify TMRCA overlap between transmission classes. Left panel: distributions of pairwise cophenetic distances (from IQ-TREE maximum-likelihood tree) for directly and non-directly transmitted pairs under baseline simulation parameters. Right panel: same distributions under the recombination sweep condition. Overlap between classes is substantially greater in cophenetic distances than in the corresponding TMRCA distributions (Figure 1), demonstrating that the phylogenetic representation loses genealogical resolution as recombination increases. This mechanistically explains the earlier and steeper failure of phylogenetic relative to IBD-based approaches at the detection threshold.

*Supplementary Figure S6* provides mean AUPRC values with 95% confidence intervals as line plots across the full recombination rate range for all three methods under each migration condition, offering a continuous characterisation of the performance gradient that complements the threshold-focused presentation of Section 3. The progressive separation of method-specific AUPRC trajectories with increasing recombination rate is visible here, and the point at which phylogenetic AUPRC diverges from IBD AUPRC aligns with the transition between the phase regimes identified in Figure 3A.

## Section 5

### Population structure modulates but cannot override the recombination-governed detectability ceiling

Having established that the detection threshold is governed primarily by recombination rate and grounded in coalescent geometry, we examined whether population structure — specifically the rate of parasite migration between sampling locations — could meaningfully shift the detectability ceiling. This is a practically important question for surveillance design, because migration rates are determined by the epidemiological context in which a genomic surveillance programme operates and cannot be controlled by programme design.

Across all three migration rates tested (m = 0.001, 0.01, and 0.05, representing low, intermediate, and high gene flow between sampling locations), AUPRC increased monotonically with recombination rate for all three inference approaches, and the threshold-like transition identified in Section 3 was preserved at the same recombination rate regardless of migration condition (Figure 5A–D). The quantitative effect of migration on AUPRC within any recombination stratum was at most 0.024 AUPRC units across the full range of migration values tested — an effect size approximately one order of magnitude smaller than the recombination-driven AUPRC range across the full parameter sweep (Figure 5E). No migration condition displaced any approach from the non-identifiable to the identifiable regime at recombination rates below the detection threshold, and no migration condition suppressed performance into the non-identifiable regime at recombination rates above it.

The direction of the migration effect was consistent across methods and recombination conditions: higher migration rates produced marginally higher AUPRC, particularly at recombination rates above the detection threshold. This pattern is mechanistically interpretable. Higher migration rates increase the frequency of non-local parasite lineages in any given sampling location, which increases the genetic diversity of the background population and thereby sharpens the contrast between transmission-linked pairs (which share recent ancestry from local transmission chains) and unrelated pairs (which increasingly reflect divergent ancestry from distant subpopulations). This mild improvement, however, operates only within the region of parameter space where detectable signal already exists, and its magnitude is insufficient to alter the fundamental structure of the detectability landscape.

> **Figure 5.** Migration rate modulates but does not govern the detectability ceiling. **(A–D)** Mean AUPRC (± 95% CI) as a function of recombination rate for IBD (A), IBS (B), and Phylo (C) at three migration rates (m = 0.001, red; m = 0.01, orange; m = 0.05, yellow), and the best AUPRC across methods at each parameter combination (D). The detection threshold location is invariant to migration. **(E)** AUPRC gradient across the full recombination × migration space, showing that migration-driven displacement (≤ 0.024 AUPRC units) is an order of magnitude smaller than the recombination-driven range across the tested sweep. The dominant gradient runs along the recombination axis in all panels.

These findings have a direct implication for the interpretation of genomic surveillance results. The detectability ceiling characterised here is a property of the parasite biological system — specifically, its recombination rate and the resulting genomic relatedness landscape — rather than a demographic property of the host-population context in which transmission occurs. Surveillance programmes operating in settings with varying transmission intensity, geographical connectivity, or seasonal structure will therefore encounter qualitatively similar detectability constraints as long as the parasite recombination rate remains within the empirically observed range for P. falciparum.

## Section 6

### Surveillance design shapes detectability within the biological boundary but cannot transcend it

The results in Sections 3–5 establish that the detectability ceiling is governed primarily by recombination rate and is only marginally sensitive to population structure. We next examined whether surveillance design choices — how many samples to collect and over what temporal depth of transmission to aggregate them — can improve detection within the biologically constrained parameter space. Both sample size and generational window depth are within the control of a surveillance programme, making their interaction with the detectability ceiling of direct operational relevance.

#### 6.1 Sample size interacts with recombination rate to define the achievable detectability ceiling

Best AUPRC — the maximum performance achievable across all inference approaches at each parameter combination — was computed as a function of sample size (n = 100, 200, 400) and recombination rate under three migration conditions (Figure 6). The resulting heatmaps revealed two features of the detectability landscape that together define the boundary of high-confidence transmission detection.

First, the region of high detectability (AUPRC \> 0.80) was confined to the intersection of the highest recombination rate tested (r = $10^{-6}$) and the smallest sample size (n = 100), and disappeared entirely at n ≥ 200 under most migration conditions. This contraction with increasing sample size appears counterintuitive — more samples might be expected to improve statistical power — but reflects a dilution mechanism that is a direct consequence of the class imbalance structure of the detection problem. As sample size increases, the number of all possible pairs grows as n(n-1)/2, whereas the number of transmission-linked pairs grows more slowly, determined by the underlying transmission intensity rather than by the sampling fraction. The ratio of unrelated pairs to transmission- linked pairs therefore increases with sampling depth, reducing precision at any fixed recall level and lowering AUPRC. This sample size paradox is not a statistical artefact: it reflects the fact that broad population surveillance, by design, collects more background pairs relative to transmission signal, pushing conditions further into the low-precision region of the precision-recall space.

Second, the AUPRC gradient across the heatmap ran primarily along the recombination axis rather than the sample size axis, confirming that the choice of sample size operates within a detectability range already defined by parasite biology. At r = $10^{-9}$ and r = $10^{-8}$, best AUPRC remained below 0.62 regardless of sample size or migration condition, consistent with the hard floor established in Section 3. At r = 6.67 × $10^{-7}$ (the empirically relevant regime for P. falciparum), achievable AUPRC ranged from approximately 0.67–0.75 depending on sample size, with smaller samples performing better within this range.

> **Figure 6.** Maximum achievable detectability across surveillance parameter space. Heatmaps of best AUPRC (maximum across all inference approaches) as a function of recombination rate (rows) and sample size (columns) under migration rates of m = 0.001 (A), m = 0.01 (B), and m = 0.05 (C). Colour scale: dark blue = low AUPRC (≤ 0.55); yellow = high AUPRC (≥ 0.80). The AUPRC \> 0.80 region is confined to small sample sizes at the highest recombination rate and is absent at n ≥ 200. The dominant gradient runs along the recombination axis in all three panels. The "best AUPRC" framing reflects the maximum attainable performance from any approach — characterising the ceiling of the detectable parameter space rather than the performance of any particular method.

*Supplementary Figure S5* provides the IBD advantage gap — the difference between IBD AUPRC and the next-best method — across the same sample size × recombination space. This gap is most pronounced at the extremes of the recombination range: at r = $10^{-9}$, IBD's ability to detect long shared segments provides an advantage of 0.158–0.176 AUPRC units over IBS, narrowing to 0.049–0.081 at intermediate rates and widening again to 0.085–0.095 at r = $10^{-6}$. The non-monotonic profile of this advantage reflects the differential sensitivity of segment-based inference to recombination-driven genealogical fragmentation and is reported here as a secondary characterisation; the primary finding of Section 6.1 remains the structure of the best-AUPRC landscape irrespective of method. *Supplementary Table S4* provides complete best-AUPRC values across all sample size, recombination rate, and migration combinations.

#### 6.2 Generational window depth and recombination rate interact to govern the temporal boundary of detectable transmission signal

Genomic surveillance programmes typically sample parasites across a defined period of active transmission. The generational depth of the aggregated sample — how many rounds of transmission are represented by the collected pairs — affects detectability by controlling the ratio of recent to older transmission links within the pair pool. We examined how the generational depth threshold (G, from G = 1 to G = 25) interacted with recombination rate to determine AUPRC for each inference approach (Figure 7).

Across all methods and recombination conditions, extending the generational window improved detection performance above the recombination threshold. At r = 6.67 × $10^{-7}$, expanding the window from G = 5 to G = 25 improved best AUPRC by approximately 0.08–0.12 units, with the gain concentrated between G = 5 and G = 15 and diminishing above G = 20 (Figure 7D). This improvement occurs because broader generational windows include more transmission-linked pairs in the positive class, increasing the absolute number of detectable events that contribute to precision and recall estimates. Below the recombination threshold (r ≤ $10^{-8}$), generational window expansion produced no improvement for any approach: the AUPRC gain curve was flat across all G values, confirming that temporal aggregation cannot compensate for the absence of sufficient recombination-driven genealogical fragmentation.

The three inference approaches showed divergent responses to generational window expansion at recombination rates above the threshold (Figure 7A–C). IBD-based inference improved monotonically with increasing G and retained its performance advantage across the full window range. IBS showed similar but slightly smaller gains. Phylogenetic cophenetic distance, by contrast, showed degradation of AUPRC with increasing G at low recombination rates — performance that was already marginal at G = 5 worsened as the window expanded. This deterioration reflects the accumulation of genealogically complex pairs within the positive class at larger G values: at G = 20–25, the positive class includes pairs separated by many transmission steps, whose genealogical relationship to sampled parasites is mediated through long recombination histories that dilute tree-level signal. IBD segment detection, operating on local genomic windows, is less sensitive to this dilution because it detects whatever shared blocks remain at any given locus regardless of the complexity of the intervening pedigree.

> **Figure 7.** Generational window depth and recombination rate interact to govern the temporal boundary of detectable transmission signal. **(A–C)** Heatmaps of AUPRC as a function of G threshold (x-axis) and recombination rate (y-axis) for IBD (A), IBS (B), and Phylo (C). Colour scale as in Figure 6. At high recombination, AUPRC improves with increasing G for IBD and IBS but is stable or declining for Phylo. At low recombination, all heatmaps are uniformly dark (AUPRC ≤ 0.55), confirming that G expansion cannot rescue detection below the biological threshold. **(D)** G-threshold gain curves: mean AUPRC as a function of G for all three methods at the empirically relevant recombination rate (r = 6.67 × $10^{-7}$, solid lines) and at below-threshold recombination (r = $10^{-8}$, dashed lines). The gain from G = 5 to G = 25 at the empirical rate is approximately 0.08–0.12 AUPRC units; gain at the below-threshold rate is indistinguishable from zero for all methods.

Taken together, the results of Sections 6.1 and 6.2 define the practical operating envelope for malaria genomic surveillance. Transmission detection can be improved by collecting smaller, more targeted samples (which reduces dilution) and by aggregating across broader transmission windows (which increases the positive pair yield above the recombination threshold). Neither strategy, however, extends the detectable parameter space below the biological floor identified in Section 3. The actionable space for surveillance design is not the recombination rate — which is fixed by parasite biology — but the combination of sampling strategy and temporal window that maximises the signal-to-noise ratio within the biologically constrained regime.
